You Can See Everything Review

I saw You Can See Everything in theaters today. I thought it was artsy and thought-provoking. I wanted to jot down some thoughts on it, so this post contains heavy spoilers for the film.

Signs and Symbols

In 1981, Jean Baudrillard wrote an influential essay titled Simulacra and Simulation in which he argues that modern society has replaced reality with symbols and signs. He outlines four sequential stages:

  1. a faithful image of reality
  2. a perversion of reality
  3. masking of the absence of a profound reality
  4. a pure simulacrum with no basis in true reality, a hyperreality

The film can be analyzed by roughly splitting it into these four phases. The conceit of the film is a window into Elizabeth Holmes, the disgraced CEO of Theranos; a faithful image of reality which Holmes and co hope will change the perception of her as a dishonest liar through unfiltered, honest depiction. Throughout the first act, the figures involved spend a lot of time thinking and talking about honesty and communication, and it seems really difficult to get a clear answer from anybody about anything. As the characters get to know each other, it becomes fairly apparent that despite being fully embedded in the house, Nathan can't actually peer inside their minds, he can't see anything, and that faithful image still seems deceitful and perverse.

Holmes vanishes for prison about halfway through the film, and as a result the film largely becomes about recreating her in her absence. Billy Evans and Nathan Fielder both spend much of the second act attempting to puppet various simulacra which are intended to serve as stand-ins for the real Elizabeth Holmes. Billy Evans creates an AI-powered hologram which he can direct like a marionette, and uses power of attorney to gain access to her X account to impersonate her. Likewise, Nathan Fielder gets Amanda Seyfried to reprise her role as Holmes, and spends much of the film nitpicking her performances to make her act more like Holmes, even making slight changes to the script when her interpretation doesn't align with his.

As the film nears the end, it begins layering parallels on top of parallels. Seyfried is shocked that Holmes is upset about her portrayal in The Dropout, and claims that her project was well-researched, actually, and that she was just doing her job, which entitled her to take artistic liberties. Seyfried also accuses Nathan of caring too much about Holmes, being unable to remain objective due to his attachment. He mentions not bringing his emotions into his work, and the image cuts to him swimming in the ocean with Holmes.

The film ends on the note that Amy Chozick both cannot confirm and stands by the Holmes quote: "They're not playing me. They're playing a character I created." This quote might be fabricated, perhaps as a dramatization of something someone said at some point. The quote acts as a depiction of a depiction of Holmes, a referent to a referent, a copy of a copy of a copy. At this point in the film we are not watching a faithful image of reality, we're not even watching a perversion of that reality. We're attempting to navigate through the depiction of a character who has not spoken in the film for well over 90 minutes, told through increasingly emotionally invested men. A hyperreality.

The Ouroboros of Deception Has No Beginning

An interesting absence in this film is Sunny Balwani, the president of Theranos who was also implicated during this whole situation. He is only really mentioned from time to time, in passing, mostly from people in Holmes's orbit who blame him for everything that went wrong. This is a prominent narrative in the film: Holmes's icky boyfriend was manipulating her, it's all his fault, if only he were not involved!

But the second act of the film is where things start to get really twisted. After two years pass, Billy Evans seems to have gone completely nuts. He somehow gains power of attorney over Holmes, he's impersonating her, he's even claiming that he knows what she wants without needing to consult her. Frankly, he seems suspiciously way more icky and manipulative than he did in the first act, partially buttressed by the 2024 tech-right Silicon Valley social media "vibe shift" visibly transforming his personality.

Never explicitly stated here is the clear organizational parallel to Sunny Balwani at Theranos, another icky manipulative guy Holmes dated before. Like Evans, Balwani was famously obsessed with attempting to appear smarter or higher-status, famously overbearing and paranoid. A major part of the Theranos lawsuit was Holmes's accusations that he was controlling and abusive. Here we have precisely that same narrative, woven together through this film depicting Billy Evans as unbearably controlling, going to great lengths to highlight how he just couldn't resist taking the egg whisking task from her.

Some of this is storytelling sleight-of-hand by Fielder. The first act we see him mostly seeming lured in by Holmes, being used by her, with subtle signs that he might be a bit controlling and overprotective. It's later revealed in dramatizated flashbacks that he was pretty manipulative before Holmes went to prison, as well, trying to secure clandestine financial compensation for agreeing to the Holmes documentary. It's a point of confusion that never gets really resolved throughout the film: was he directed to do this by Holmes, or was he doing it behind her back? Is she a clueless victim? Is she the most manipulative person imaginable?

This question isn't resolved, and the viewer, like Fielder, is left totally lost at sea trying to navigate them. It's no secret that Holmes admired Steve Jobs, who very very famously had a level of charisma that was described as a reality distortion field. Fielder is visibly confused about what is happening throughout the film, caught in this web of confusion, right up until the moment Holmes gets locked up in prison, after which the movie immediately has a dramatic shift to a completely normal Nathan Fielder piece about Billy Evans – Evans is super easy to understand in comparison, and will quickly talk himself into a corner just like so many of Fielder's subjects over the years.

But this, too, is deception. Fielder can trivially manipulate the audience to find Evans reprehensible, and does so easily. But this only happens after this competing distortion field is removed, and it feels sort of empty with the absence of Holmes, who is the actual story here. The "origin" of the deception is not very important to the film, because the real object of the film isn't litigating the deception, it's trying to stumble through this field, like a funhouse of mirrors, where sincerity is so hard to determine that it might as well not exist.
